## Summary
Nolan argues that "financial repression" — the government's quiet strategy of holding real interest rates below inflation to inflate away debt — is already active in 2026 and poses a serious threat to anyone holding cash or bonds. He walks through three forms it takes in a typical investor's portfolio (rate suppression, pension tilt, tolerance drift), then prescribes a three-move defense: real asset anchor (dividend ETFs + broad index), a TIPS ladder, and a high-yield savings account as a liquidity cushion.

## The Setup
- US national debt is ~$39 trillion; public debt-to-GDP ~100% — almost exactly the level at the end of World War II in 1946.
- From 1946 to 1974, the US used financial repression to drop debt-to-GDP from ~106% to ~24%, liquidating roughly 3–4% of GDP per year via negative real rates.
- A saver in 1946 holding $100,000 in cash would have had only ~$39,500 of real purchasing power by 1974. Rolling 3-month T-bills the whole time still produced a 16% real loss. The S&P 500 with dividends reinvested produced ~$520,000 real — 5x the starting value.
- Janet Yellen in January 2026: "Fiscal dominance is also likely to raise term premia and borrowing costs as investors become concerned that the government will rely on inflation or financial repression to manage its debt."

## Key Analysis

### Form 1: Rate Suppression (Real Yield Bleed)
- Fed funds rate: 3.5–3.75%
- Most recent CPI: 3.8% year-over-year
- Fed policy rate is already negative in real terms
- FDIC national average savings rate (May 2026): 0.45% APY → real yield = **negative 2.4%**
- Best high-yield savings accounts: ~3.2–3.5% APY → real yield approximately zero
- Dollar math at 3.3% CPI over 5 years:
  - $100,000 regular savings → loses ~$13,700 real purchasing power
  - $250,000 regular savings → loses ~$34,000 real
  - $500,000 regular savings → loses ~$68,000 real

### Form 2: Pension Tilt
- Regulatory frameworks (capital requirements, pension laws) force large pools — banks, pension funds, insurance companies, target-date retirement funds — to hold low-yield Treasuries regardless of real return.
- Target-date funds glide from stock-heavy in your 20s to 40–60% bonds by age 65; most of that bond exposure is intermediate and long-duration Treasuries.
- 10-year Treasury yield: ~4.4%; 30-year: ~5.0%; CPI: 3.8% → real yield on 10-year is under 1%; real yield on 30-year ~1%.
- If inflation drifts to 4% without a Fed response, those bonds generate a real loss inside your retirement account.
- Dollar example: $100,000 in a typical 65-year-old target-date fund earns ~$7,000 real on the bond half over 5 years at current rates; if inflation drifts to 4%, that turns to a loss of $1,000–$2,000 real per year.

### Form 3: Tolerance Drift
- The Fed's official 2% inflation target has not been consistently hit since 2021; CPI has run 3–9% since then, and currently sits at 3.8%.
- Nolan's thesis: the Fed may simply stop fighting to get back to 2% ("tolerance drift") because cutting rates harder risks economic damage and because fighting inflation costs the Treasury hundreds of billions in additional interest expense.
- Ben Bernanke's 2002 "printing press" speech cited: the Fed has unlimited ability to fight deflation but limited political will to fight inflation.
